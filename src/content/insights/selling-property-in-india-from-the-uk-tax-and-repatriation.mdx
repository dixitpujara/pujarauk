## Two tax systems, one sale

If you live in the UK and sell a flat, house or plot in India, the sale is taxed in India first, because the property is there. It is then reportable in the UK, because you are UK resident. Done well, you pay tax once. Done badly, money sits frozen in an Indian bank account, too much tax is withheld at source, and the UK return is filed with figures nobody can support.

## Step one: understand your status in India

For Indian tax purposes you are almost certainly a non-resident (an NRI, or a person of Indian origin holding an OCI card). That status drives three things: the rate at which the buyer must withhold tax, which bank account the proceeds can be paid into, and the forms needed to move the money out of India.

Your Indian PAN (permanent account number) must be active and linked to your current details. Without one, nothing else can proceed.

## Step two: the buyer deducts tax at source

When a non-resident sells property in India, the buyer is required to deduct tax at source (TDS) from the sale price and pay it to the Indian government before paying you the balance. The deduction is calculated on the full sale consideration, not on your gain, and for a non-resident seller the rate is far higher than the one percent that applies when the seller is resident. In practice this means a large slice of the price can be withheld even if your actual gain is modest or nil.

## Step three: apply for a lower or nil deduction certificate

You do not have to accept that. Before the sale completes, a non-resident seller can apply to the Indian tax authorities for a certificate authorising the buyer to deduct tax at a lower rate, or at nil, based on the actual expected gain. The application sets out the purchase cost, improvement costs, the expected sale price and the resulting gain. The certificate is issued to the buyer and seller for that specific transaction.

Timing is everything. The application takes several weeks, and the certificate has to be in hand before the buyer pays. Agree the sale, apply for the certificate, then complete. If the sale completes first, the only route to the excess tax is a refund claim through an Indian tax return, which is slower.

## Step four: work out the capital gain

Indian capital gains tax distinguishes between short-term and long-term gains on immovable property, with the holding period deciding which applies. Long-term gains have historically benefited from indexation, which adjusts the purchase cost for inflation. The rules on indexation and the applicable rates were changed in July 2024, with transitional treatment for property acquired before that change. [PLACEHOLDER: confirm current indexation and rate rules]

The figures you need to assemble:

- the original purchase deed and price, and the date of purchase
- stamp duty and registration costs paid at purchase
- documented improvement costs, with receipts
- selling costs such as brokerage and legal fees
- the sale deed and the price actually received

Inherited and gifted property has its own rules for cost and holding period, generally taking the previous owner's cost and dates. Keep the chain of documents: the inheritance certificate, will or gift deed, and the earlier purchase deed.

Certain reinvestment reliefs can reduce or eliminate the Indian gain if the proceeds are reinvested in specified assets within set time limits. Whether these help a UK resident depends on the UK treatment too, so they should be looked at on both sides before relying on them.

## Step five: file the Indian return

Even if the certificate was granted and tax was deducted correctly, you will usually need to file an Indian income tax return for the year of sale. The return reports the gain, claims credit for the TDS already paid, and either settles the balance or claims a refund. The Indian tax year runs from 1 April to 31 March, and returns for individuals are generally due by 31 July following the year end. [PLACEHOLDER: confirm the due date for the relevant year]

## Step six: repatriate the money

Sale proceeds paid to a non-resident go into an NRO (non-resident ordinary) account in India. To move funds from an NRO account to the UK you need:

- Form 15CB, a certificate from an Indian chartered accountant confirming the tax position of the remittance
- Form 15CA, the online declaration filed with the Indian tax department before the bank will process the transfer
- the bank's own remittance paperwork, including the sale deed and proof of the source of funds

Under the Reserve Bank of India's rules, a non-resident can repatriate up to a stated annual limit from an NRO account, currently USD 1 million per financial year [check current RBI limit], covering sale proceeds and other balances. Larger amounts can be spread across financial years or may need specific approval.

## The UK side

UK residents are taxed on their worldwide gains, so the Indian property sale has to be reported on your UK Self Assessment return for the tax year in which the sale completed, converted to sterling at the appropriate exchange rates. The UK calculates its own gain under UK rules, using UK reliefs and the UK annual exempt amount, so the UK figure will not match the Indian one.

For people who were not UK domiciled, the remittance basis was abolished from 6 April 2025 and replaced by a regime based on years of UK residence, under which qualifying new arrivals can receive foreign income and gains free of UK tax for their first four years. [PLACEHOLDER: confirm details] Whether that applies to you depends on when you came to the UK and your residence history, and it has to be claimed.

The UK-India Double Taxation Convention prevents the same gain being taxed twice. In most cases the UK gives a foreign tax credit for Indian tax paid on the gain, up to the amount of UK tax due on the same gain. The credit is claimed on the UK return, so you need the Indian assessment and the TDS certificates to support it. If the Indian tax is higher than the UK tax, the excess cannot be refunded by HMRC.

## Common mistakes

- Completing the sale before applying for the lower deduction certificate.
- Letting the buyer deduct TDS without obtaining the TDS certificate that proves it was paid.
- Paying the proceeds into an NRE or resident account by mistake, which complicates repatriation.
- Reporting the sale in the wrong UK tax year because the Indian and UK years do not align.
- Forgetting that the UK return needs sterling figures at the right dates, not the rupee figures.
